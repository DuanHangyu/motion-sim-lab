How a rocket nozzle makes thrust

A rocket moves forward by throwing gas backward, very fast. Burning propellant in the chamber makes hot gas at high pressure (right now 7.0 MPa, 69 times the air's, at 3,500 K). The gas can only leave through the nozzle, and the nozzle's job is to turn that heat and pressure into speed.

Narrow, then wide

In the chamber the gas crawls (200 m/s). It speeds up as the passage narrows and reaches exactly the speed of sound, Mach 1, at the narrowest point, the throat (1,200 m/s in gas this hot). Past the throat something counter-intuitive happens: because the flow is now faster than sound, a widening passage makes it go faster still. It expands, cools and drops in pressure, leaving at Mach 3.3 and 2.87 km/s. Drag the glowing parcel (or the chart on the left) to ride along and read the speed, pressure and temperature at every point.

Where the push lands

Turn on Pressure: the hot gas presses on every wall. On the chamber's closed end and on the bell it pushes the engine forward; on the narrowing section it pushes back. Add it up and subtract the air pressing on the outside, and you get the thrust, exactly the momentum the exhaust carries away (Momentum). The engine is never pushing on the air behind it: in vacuum, with no air at all, it pushes harder.

The throat and the plume

The throat's size against the fixed exit sets how far the gas expands. Leave too much pressure in the exhaust (a wide throat, or high altitude) and it bursts outward after the exit: the plume flares. Expand it too far below the air's pressure (a narrow throat at sea level) and the air squeezes the jet: the plume pinches, and bright shock diamonds appear where the squeezed flow is shocked back up to chamber heat. Too narrow and the flow tears off the bell's wall. The triangle under the throat slider marks the ideal throat for the air outside.

What is exact

Quasi-one-dimensional isentropic flow of a hot gas (γ = 1.2, 3,500 K, like kerosene and oxygen) through this engine's real contour: the Mach number, pressure, temperature, speed, mass flow and thrust are computed, not drawn. The plume's shape, its shock spacing and its colours are approximations built on those numbers, and the gas is shown slowed down 2,000 times.
