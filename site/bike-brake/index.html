Stop, and still steer

A front wheel with a disc brake spins on a brake tester: the drum under the tyre is the road. Behind it a model road runs the same test at , with a rock in the lane past the brake line. The rider grabs one lever at the line and tries to swerve round the rock.

Why a locked wheel cannot steer

A tyre has one budget of grip, about μ × the load on it. Rolling, it can spend that budget slowing the bike and turning it at the same time. Brake so hard that the wheel stops and the tyre slides, and all its grip points straight back along the slide: none is left for turning. A locked front runs straight on, into the rock, and with no grip to steer under the bike it cannot balance either. A locked rear skids and swings, but the front still rolls and steers; it just takes far longer, because braking throws the load off the rear. On dry asphalt a hard front brake grips harder than the bike can tip, so the rear lifts and you go over the bars before the tyre ever locks.

What ABS does

It watches the wheel. The moment the tyre slips past its peak grip, or the rear starts to lift, it dumps the pressure, lets the wheel spin back up and squeezes again, times a second here. The tyre stays near its peak: about the shortest stop the grip allows, with grip left over to steer. On ice even that is not enough.

Try

Front lock on wet, then ABS. Drag the speed up until nothing avoids the rock. Put it on ice. Tap the tyre to brake again, the drum to change the grip, the padlock and the ABS ring on the console to change the brake, and drag the console's knobs.
