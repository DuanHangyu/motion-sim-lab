How the compass field lab works

Under the glass lies a bar magnet: red is its north end, blue its south. Around it is a magnetic field, a push and twist that fills the space but that you cannot see. This page makes it visible three ways.

The compass draws it

A compass needle is a small magnet on a pivot. The field twists it until it lines up, so its red end points the way the field goes: out of north poles and into south poles. It overshoots and swings back before it settles, faster where the field is strong. Drag the compass anywhere on the glass; when you let go it stamps an arrow, the way you would mark a sheet of paper in a school lab. Trace walks it along its own needle, one needle-length at a time, and the dots it leaves are a field line.

The filings reveal the pattern

Iron filings become tiny magnets in the field. They turn along it and cling end to end in chains, so when you sprinkle them and tap the glass (click it) they gather into lines. The filings show the lines but not their direction: only the compass knows which way is north.

Two magnets add up

Every magnet's field adds to the others' at each point. Choose Attract, Repel or Side, or drag the second magnet anywhere (drag its end to turn it, click it to flip it). Between two like poles the fields cancel at a neutral point: there only the Earth's weak field is left, and the needle drifts lazily back to north. Press Guess, aim the amber arrow where you think the needle will point (drag on the glass), then press Reveal.

What is exact and what is not

Each magnet is modelled as a uniformly magnetised ferrite bar (10 × 1.6 × 1.6 cm), whose field is that of magnetic charge spread on its two end faces, plus a 20 µT Earth field pointing north. The field, the needle's swing and the numbers in the card are computed from that. The chains the filings form, the fall of the sprinkled grains and the needle's weight are simplified or slowed so you can see them; the README lists each one.
