How a speaker makes sound

A signal generator sends a changing voltage down the cables. In the speaker it becomes a current in the voice coil, a coil of copper wire that sits in the narrow gap of a strong magnet. Current across a magnetic field makes a force (F = B·l·i), so the coil is pushed forward or pulled back, and it drags the cone with it. The spider and the rubber surround are springs that centre the cone and pull it home.

The cone pushes on the air in front of it. Air is springy: each push squeezes a layer, that layer squeezes the next, and the squeeze travels away at the speed of sound. Those travelling squeezes are the rings you see; the dots are air, bunching up where the pressure is high.

What to try

Scrub: drag along the waveform on the console, on the generator or in the card (or turn the big jog knob) and the cone and the air follow that exact moment. Switch between a low bass, a pure tone and a sung voice with the console's three buttons. Notice the cone's travel: bass needs about 36 times more than the tone for a similar loudness, because slow pushes barely squeeze the air. Push the cone with the pointer: a slow push makes almost no ring, a quick flick makes a click. Only the cone's acceleration reaches your ears.

What is real and what is not

The numbers are those of a real 8-inch woofer driven by 1 watt, worked out from its measured-style parameters every step: the coil current, the force, the cone's travel and the pressure a metre away. To make it visible, time runs 200× slower, sound travels a further 15× slower (so its rings sit 15× closer together than in real air), the cone's motion is drawn enlarged (the factor is under the travel), and the rings are drawn in a cone either side of the axis (in real air, at these pitches, they carry on all round the room). The sound you can switch on is the same signal at real speed.
