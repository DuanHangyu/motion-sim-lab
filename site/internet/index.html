    How the Internet works

    This is one page load, from the moment you press Enter to the moment the page is drawn, slowed down until you can follow each packet. The world is a model: distances are squeezed so a street and an ocean fit on one table. The clock is not squeezed. Every time you see comes from the real length of each cable and the speed of light in glass.

    The four acts

    1. The address (DNS). Names are for people; machines need a number. Your provider’s resolver asks a root server, then the server for the last part of the name (.net), then the site’s own name server.
    2. The connection (TCP and TLS). Two handshakes, each a round trip across the ocean: one to agree to talk and number the bytes, one to agree on a secret key in the open (the paint trick) and check the server’s certificate.
    3. The request. One packet, followed hop by hop: radio to your router, which swaps your private address for your home’s public one; light in a fibre down the street; your provider’s routers; the internet exchange where networks meet; a cable on the ocean floor; a data center.
    4. The answer. The page comes back as 28 packets. TCP sends 10, waits for the receipts, then 20. One is dropped by a busy router and sent again. The browser builds the page as it arrives and asks for its styles, script and photo on the same connection.

    What you can do

    Play or drag the rail to move through the half-second; the clock shows the real time, and how much it is slowed down. Type any site in the address bar: the page asks a real DNS server for its address and replays the trip with it. The what-if switches recompute everything: put the site in your city (a CDN: no ocean, 8 times faster), cut the ocean cable (traffic takes a longer detour), turn off encryption (one round trip less, and every machine on the way can read you), or let the resolver remember the address. Click any building to jump to its chapter. The inspector on the right shows the packet you are following, layer by layer, as it changes at each hop.

    Reading the screen

    The strip at the top is the trip as a line of stops: the dot is your packet, with the time since it left and the distance so far. On the right, Packet shows the packet you are following, layer by layer, and Timeline draws every packet as a line from one side to the other with time running down, the way network engineers draw it: the steeper the line, the longer the trip. Glass panels in the scene show what each machine is doing as it does it: the router’s address table, the street splitter’s time slots, the provider’s routing lookup, the DNS walk with the real records, the exchange’s route choice, the server at work, and where the milliseconds go.

    What is exact, and what is not

    Exact: the order of every packet, the handshakes (TCP, TLS 1.3), TCP’s slow start and its recovery from a lost packet, the speed of light in fibre (204,000 km/s) and every cable’s delay from its length: 6,000 km of ocean is 29.4 ms each way. Typical, not measured: the waits (about a millisecond for Wi-Fi to find a quiet moment, half a millisecond for your fibre’s time slot, 12 ms for the server to make the page). Simplified: one path, one lost packet, all three DNS servers in your city, one data center. Real pages also make dozens of requests; this one makes four.
