    What happens when you send a prompt

    One real question, followed from the keyboard to a GPU and back, slowed down until every step can be seen. The film is scrubbable: drag the rail, jump between chapters, or change the run with the switches.

    The run

    The question “Why is the sky blue?” was sent to gpt-oss-120b, the open-weight model OpenAI released in August 2025, through OpenRouter on 1 October 2026, with the app instruction “Answer in one short sentence.”, reasoning set to low and temperature 1.0 (the model’s recommended setting). It thought “We must answer in one short sentence.” and answered “Because molecules in the atmosphere scatter shorter blue wavelengths of sunlight more than other colors.”

    What is real

    • The template the model reads (its “harmony” format) and all 86 tokens of it, with their numbers, from the model’s own tokenizer (o200k_harmony). “Split your own text” uses the same list, live, in your browser.
    • The model’s thinking and its answer, token for token; the top-20 odds behind every word of the answer, as the API returned them. The coin flip between in and scatter (50% each) is real.
    • The two other runs behind the switches: the same request with no randomness (temperature 0) and with high reasoning (225 tokens of notes before a shorter answer).
    • The model’s design: 36 layers, 2,880 numbers per token, 64 attention heads sharing 8 key-value heads, alternating full and 128-token attention, 128 experts per layer with 4 used, 116.8 billion weights of which 5.1 billion work on each token, 60.8 GiB on disk (OpenAI’s model card). The GPU: an H100 SXM, 80 GB of HBM3 at 3.35 TB/s.

    What is modelled

    • The timing. 24 ms each way on the network; a gateway, a 6 ms safety check and a short queue; then the GPU’s steps, from a memory model: each step reads the weights it needs once for everyone in the batch (attention and embedding weights, plus every expert any token in the step uses: 128·(1−(1−4/128)n) for n tokens) and the conversations’ caches, at 80% of the chip’s bandwidth, plus 1.2 ms of overhead; arithmetic at 45% of peak when it takes longer. With 32 conversations sharing the chip that gives about 18 ms a token.
    • The energy. The GPU at ~600 W while it works, your share of each step, doubled for the rest of the server, the network and cooling.

    What is drawn for illustration

    • Which 4 experts each token visits, the attention lines (patterns of the kinds real heads learn), the numbers drawn inside the windows and in the “2,880 numbers” strip: this run’s internals were not recorded, only its inputs, outputs and odds.
    • The city. Seen up close a GPU is not a city of towers: its compute units work on every conversation in turn. The towers stand for the conversations’ caches, the part of memory each one holds; their windows, one per token per layer, are what the model keeps. The other conversations are invented and drawn narrower than they would be.
    • The ring of bars round the wheel: the top 20 are the real odds; the rest stands in for the other 201,068 tokens.

    Simplified

    • Big services split a model across several GPUs, or use other chips, and batch far more cleverly; their queues and safety layers differ. Frontier models are larger than this one, but every step here is the same kind of step.
    • The trip across the internet is compressed into one chapter; it has its own film: How the Internet Works.
