Two wheels, one engine

A car's engine turns one shaft, but its two driven wheels cannot always turn together. In a corner the inner wheel runs a tighter circle than the outer wheel, so in the same time it covers less ground. Bolt both wheels to one axle and one of them has to skid. The differential is the box of gears in the middle that lets them turn at different speeds while the engine drives both.

What you are looking at

The engine (the motor on the arm above) turns the pinion. The pinion turns the big gold ring gear, which is bolted to the carrier, so the carrier spins with it. Inside the carrier a pin holds two small spider gears. They mesh with two side gears, one on each axle. Each wheel stands on its own rolling road: a belt that moves the way the ground would move under that wheel.

Why it works

Driving straight, the spider gears do not turn on their pin at all: they carry both side gears round like a solid block, and both wheels turn at the ring gear's speed. In a turn, one side gear has to slow down and the other speed up. The spider gears roll between them, spinning on their pin, so the ring gear always turns at the average of the two wheels: 120 and 180 rpm average to 150. Because the spider gears push on both side gears equally, the torque is always split 50 / 50.

The catch, and the lock

50 / 50 also means that the wheel with less grip decides how much torque the other can have. Put one wheel on ice and it spins at twice the ring gear's speed, while the wheel on dry road stands still: the car goes nowhere. The diff lock joins the two sides, so the wheels must turn together and the torque goes to whichever wheel can use it. The car climbs off the ice. But in a tight turn a locked axle fights itself: one tyre has to scrub across the road, and the axle winds up like a spring.

Try this

Grab a tyre and turn it: the engine stops, the rolling roads drop away, and the other wheel turns the opposite way (the ring gear stands still, so the two wheels must average to zero). Click the lock collar on the right of the housing, click a rolling road to put it on ice, or drag the slider on the base to slow time down. Road load is how hard the road pushes back: a hill, snow, a trailer.

What is real

The numbers come from one model: a 1.5 t car, 0.33 m tyres, a 1.6 m track and a 41:11 ring and pinion (3.73 : 1). The ring gear turns at 150 rpm, which is about 19 km/h. Grip is 0.9 on dry road and 0.1 on ice, and a tyre's push is capped at grip × its load. The motion on screen plays slowed down (the slider says how much), because at full speed the gear teeth would blur into strobes; the numbers are real-time.
